# Iowa Secretary of Agriculture — Candidate Profiles

_What the office does._ The Secretary of Agriculture leads the Iowa Department of Agriculture and Land Stewardship (IDALS). The department describes its job as advocating "for Iowa's farmers" and protecting "consumers, animals, our food supply and our shared soil and water resources" [7]. In practice IDALS runs the state's voluntary, cost-share conservation programs that cut nonpoint-source (farm-field) pollution under the Iowa Nutrient Reduction Strategy. It also handles animal-disease response (for example, bird flu), food and fuel inspection, the Choose Iowa local-food program, and the state climatology bureau. The Iowa Department of Natural Resources (DNR), not IDALS, permits livestock confinements (CAFOs), regulates manure management plans and point-source discharges, and enforces pollution law [6][12]. The term is four years and the salary is $103,212 [1].

_Ballot order / candidates:_ Mike Naig (REP), Chris Jones (DEM). **Incumbent race.** Naig has held the office since 2018 and is seeking a third term [1][3].

## Mike Naig (Republican)

### At a glance
- **Age:** 48 [1]
- **Hometown:** grew up on his family's century farm near Cylinder, in Palo Alto County. Iowa PBS lists him as "R-Des Moines" [5][4][12]
- **Current role:** Iowa Secretary of Agriculture since March 2018 [1][12]
- **Education:** Buena Vista University (Storm Lake) [1][11]
- **Career:** Iowa manager of state and local government affairs for Monsanto; earlier public-policy roles with the Iowa Biotechnology Association, the Agribusiness Association of Iowa and CropLife America; deputy secretary of agriculture from September 2013 to 2018 [6][12]

### Background & career
Naig's campaign says he is "the fourth generation from his family's century farm" near Cylinder. He and his wife Jaime have three sons [11] (campaign, self-reported). Secretary Bill Northey appointed him deputy secretary in 2013. When Northey left in March 2018 for a USDA post in the first Trump administration, Gov. Kim Reynolds appointed Naig to replace him [12][20]. He won a full term in November 2018 and was re-elected in 2022 [5].

### Track record
- **Water quality / Nutrient Reduction Strategy.** IDALS works with USDA, local soil and water conservation districts and private partners on the 2013 Nutrient Reduction Strategy (NRS). The strategy uses voluntary practices such as cover crops, buffers, bioreactors and wetlands, and IDALS has used a "batch-and-build" model for edge-of-field practices [5][4]. The 2018 law SF 512 set aside about $282 million over 12 years for Water Quality Initiative projects [21]. IDALS says the state spends nearly $100 million a year on water quality. It also reports that cover crops grew from fewer than 400,000 acres a decade ago to nearly 4 million in 2024, and that more than 150 nitrate-reducing wetlands and nearly 500 nitrate-filtering buffers have been built [7]. In the Oct. 2 debate Naig said cover-crop cost-share enrollment doubled in 2026, to 1.4 million acres [4].
- **"Farm to Faucet" (2026).** Reynolds signed House File 2771, the agriculture and natural resources budget, on June 1, 2026. It redirects the state's water excise tax and makes one-time investments that IDALS estimates will total nearly $320 million over 12 years. The package includes about $52 million in new IDALS funds for practices in the 22-county Greater Des Moines watershed, $25 million to expand nitrate removal at Central Iowa Water Works, an extra $500,000 a year for DNR water monitoring, more money for small-community treatment grants (with the maximum grant raised to $1 million), and a $10 million, 1% "Rural Iowa Infrastructure Bank" for towns under 11,000 people [7]. It does not directly fund the University of Iowa river-sensor network [3]. The enrolled text (governor's letter) shows the mechanics: up to $25 million from the Iowa Finance Authority's water quality financing fund to a regional water authority serving at least 600,000 people (available through June 30, 2029) for nutrient-removal technology; $500,000 a year to DNR for monitoring; a new rural infrastructure bank with loan interest capped at 1%; and 20% of a restructured fund going to IDALS for buffers, cover crops, edge-of-field structures and grazing in six Des Moines-area watersheds (Boone, East Fork Des Moines, Middle Des Moines, North Raccoon, South Raccoon, Upper Des Moines) [28].- **Animal disease.** Naig calls Iowa's response to highly pathogenic avian influenza, an outbreak that began in 2022, one of the accomplishments he is proudest of. He cites response teams, partnerships and added funding [5]. Iowa had no commercial-flock outbreak between December 2025 and Oct. 29, 2026, when IDALS reported one; the state recorded seven outbreaks in backyard, mixed-species or game-bird flocks in 2026 [22]. IDALS's avian influenza page lists reporting and biosecurity guidance but no response statistics [30].
- **Ethanol/E15.** Naig has pushed for nationwide, year-round E15. He urged the U.S. Senate to act after the House passed a standalone bill [23], and he praised the inclusion of year-round E15 in the Senate Agriculture Committee's farm bill [9]. IDALS reported 410 million gallons of E15 sold in Iowa in 2025, up 59.7%, and estimated drivers saved about $61.5 million [8].
- **Other programs.** Launched Choose Iowa (about 360 members) and expanded the Cattle and Conservation Working Lands program to 28 counties. By his count, more than 16,000 acres have been converted to pasture [6].

### What they're running on
Naig lists his priorities as supporting the ag industry, animal health and food safety, and improving water quality and soil health [1]. He describes a "farm to faucet" approach: prevent pollutants upstream and modernize treatment downstream, rather than play the "blame game" [5]. He says "clean water is non-negotiable" [5]. If re-elected, he said he will seek more conservation funding next session and take the cattle-and-conservation program statewide [4]. In the debate he proposed a temporary fuel-tax holiday as diesel topped $6 a gallon, as well as incentives to raise Iowa ownership of farmland [2].

### Positions on key issues
- **Water regulation:** Prefers incentives and voluntary practices. Opposes new mandates and taxes on farm inputs, which he says would hit small and mid-sized farms and speed up consolidation [6].
- **Ethanol:** Calls it value-added agriculture worth about $5 billion to Iowa [3].
- **Nitrate and cancer:** At the Farm Progress Show in Boone (August), Naig said blaming farmers will not solve Iowa's cancer crisis; the Des Moines Register quotes him: "When we start to go down this path of automatically blaming someone and blaming farmers, for instance, when we really need to understand what…" (excerpt only) [29]. In an AP story he said water and cancer are "a hot topic on the campaign trail," but "we have to have a long view of these things, and we're making progress" [26]. I did not find a statement from him on whether nitrate causes cancer.- **Carbon pipeline:** Said Summit's pipeline should be built without eminent domain "if it's going to get done," and called for stronger property-rights protections [3].
- **Beef imports / land:** Called Trump's beef-import plan a "bad idea" and wants more Iowa land owned by Iowans [3][2].

### Endorsements & funding
- Iowa Farm Bureau PAC named him a 2026 "Friend of Agriculture" [16]. The Des Moines Register reported Iowa Corn Growers support [15].
- In July he reported raising more than $179,000 for the period, with more than $672,000 cash on hand [14]. Third-quarter reports showed $50,000 from the Iowa Farm Bureau PAC, $20,000 from the Koch Industries PAC and about $80,000 more from pesticide, pork, corn and ethanol donors, plus an average gift of about $1,800 from more than 300 contributors. That breakdown comes from an analysis by Food & Water Action, which has endorsed Jones [13].

### Criticisms & responses
- **Results.** Jones argues that roughly $100 million a year has not visibly improved water ("Does the water quality look a billion dollars better to you?") and calls edge-of-field practices a "Band-Aid and diaper approach" [12][5]. The Iowa Environmental Council (advocacy) earlier calculated that NRS practice adoption was too slow to meet goals for decades [21]. *Response:* Naig points to record adoption and new funding, and says Iowa is "poised" to "scale up and accelerate" [4][6].
- **Farm to Faucet.** Jones wrote that the plan requires agriculture "to be accountable NOT AT ALL" and leaves taxpayers with the cost [12]. *Response:* Naig called it a "balanced approach" that redirects existing dollars "without increasing the tax burden" [7].
- **Industry ties.** Little Village, an alt-weekly whose feature openly favored Jones's critique, and Jones call Naig a former Monsanto lobbyist and a "secretary of agribusiness" [12]. AP also notes his Monsanto background [26]. I found no direct response from Naig to the label; his campaign site (self-reported) says he has "spent his entire career working in agriculture" [11].

## Chris Jones (Democrat)

### At a glance
- **Age:** 65 [1]
- **Hometown:** grew up in Ankeny. Iowa PBS lists him as "D-Iowa City" [12][4]
- **Current role:** retired research scientist, author and water-quality advocate. First run for office [6][12]
- **Education:** bachelor's and master's degrees from Simpson College; Ph.D. in analytical chemistry from Montana State University [1][12]
- **Career:** manager of the Des Moines Water Works testing lab (eight years); environmental scientist at the Iowa Soybean Association; research engineer at the University of Iowa's IIHR (Hydroscience & Engineering) and Iowa Flood Center, 2015 to 2023; co-founder of Driftless Water Defenders (2024) [1][20][12]

### Background & career
At Des Moines Water Works, Jones managed the lab that tests the metro's source and treated water [1][20]. At the Iowa Soybean Association he set up one of the state's first independent water-monitoring programs [12]. In 2015 he joined the University of Iowa's IIHR, where he oversaw the Iowa Water Quality Information System, a statewide network of real-time river sensors [12][3]. He also wrote a public blog on water quality there. Little Village reports that in 2023 he was told two Republican state senators were upset by his writing and were threatening the sensor network's funding. He moved up his retirement to May 2023 [12]. Iowa Capital Dispatch reported the allegation at the time (May 3, 2023): Jones said Sens. Dan Zumbach and Tom Shipley pressed the university, via a lobbyist, to stop his blog by implying university funding was at risk, and that he agreed with his superiors to stop. Zumbach denied it ("No threat to funding was ever made because of the content of a blog… reckless and potentially defamatory"); Shipley did not respond; the lobbyist did not respond. The same story reported that a Senate-passed budget bill (33-16, then 62-33 in the House) took $500,000 a year from the Iowa Nutrient Research Center, which funds the sensor network [27]. The allegation remains Jones's account, denied by one of the two senators. The timing differs between sources: ICD places the cut in the 2023 session, Little Village says "the next year."

### Track record (professional; no prior elected office)
- **Writing.** His 2023 essay collection, *The Swine Republic: Struggles with the Truth about Agriculture and Water Quality*, was the Library of Congress's Iowa pick for its 2024 "Great Reads from Great Places" list [6][12]. He keeps writing at a Substack of the same name [5].
- **Advocacy.** He co-founded Driftless Water Defenders in 2024 [1].
- He has no voting or administrative record in government. That is a difference in the kind of record available, not a judgment on it.

### What they're running on
Jones calls the race "a referendum on the condition of Iowa's water" [5] and called the state of Iowa's water "the biggest failure of our state government in the history of the state" [2]. His campaign site (self-reported) has a four-point foundation and a 28-point plan [10]:
- **"Off ramps."** Taxpayer-funded training and multi-year compensation, or crop indemnification, for farmers who shift from corn and soybeans to oats, small grains, forage, fruit and vegetables, or pastured livestock [10][6][5].
- **Regulating nutrient inputs.** Rules for manure and commercial fertilizer, applied watershed by watershed across Iowa's 56 watersheds. That "will mean restrictions or taxation on fertilizer sales and animal populations" [10]. Other items: a ban on fall tillage and a tax on fall anhydrous ammonia sales [6]; no manure application from Dec. 1 to March 15; required cover crops on rented land at the landowner's expense; 50-foot stream setbacks; a moratorium on new drainage tile unless pollution at the outlet is mitigated; and a sales tax on grain-based livestock feed [10].
- **A "strategic retreat" from corn ethanol**, and returning county control over CAFO siting [10].
- **Local food.** A 10-year goal for schools to source half their food from Iowa farms, at about $10 million a year [6][2].
- Others: a clean-water constitutional amendment, more parks, and restructuring DNR environmental enforcement [10].

### Positions on key issues
- **Water regulation:** Says the voluntary approach "will not produce better water during the lifetime of anybody reading this" [10].
- **Ethanol:** Calls it an environmental and economic "dead end" [3].
- **Carbon pipeline / 45Q tax credit:** Strongly opposed [3].
- **Beef imports:** "One of the stupidest ideas I've ever heard." Wants Iowa to become "the grass-fed state" [3].
- **Land ownership:** "Iowa farmers to own their own ground." Would require conservation on rented land, in part to push absentee owners to sell [2].
- **IWILL:** Supports funding it under the 2010 voter-approved formula [4].
- **Diesel costs:** Favors a system "less reliant on fossil fuels" over a tax holiday [2].

### Endorsements & funding
- Sen. Bernie Sanders [15]; Food & Water Action (advocacy) [13].
- In July he reported raising $110,000 for the period, with about $98,500 cash on hand [14]. In the third quarter he had more than 4,000 contributions averaging about $80, about 1,700 of them $15 or less, plus $2,000 from Plumbers and Pipe Fitters Local 125's voter-education arm. That breakdown also comes from the Food & Water Action analysis [13].

### Criticisms & responses
- **"Mandates and taxes."** Naig says Jones's plan would "gut" and "shrink Iowa agriculture," and that the costs would fall hardest on small and mid-sized farms [5][6]. He called Jones an "outlier" even compared with both governor candidates [2]. The Iowa GOP (partisan) calls the 32-point plan "expensive" and "radical," with "higher taxes" and "costly mandates" [24]. *Response:* Jones calls his rules "common sense." He noted that Republican gubernatorial nominee Zach Lahn also talks about breaking up agribusiness and diversifying farms, and he says he can work with GOP legislators because rural people "bear the brunt" of pollution [2][6].
- **Earlier writing.** In 2023 ICD reported that a state lawmaker had called a 2021 Jones essay on water pollution and environmental injustice "race-baiting," and that another of his posts said Iowa's water "is polluted just as legally as black people were segregated" [27]. *Response:* Jones's essay argued that minority and low-income residents are more likely to have polluted drinking water; I found no later comment from him on the "race-baiting" charge [27].
- **Remarks about farmers.** Naig quoted Jones as saying farmers are "rich old white guys" who "only work a couple weeks a year" [6]. *Response:* Jones says he was quoted out of context. IPR published his 2021 remark that many grain farmers are "millionaires" and "only working a few weeks out of the year," made while he asked why taxpayers should "subsidize and indemnify this activity and then … pay to remediate the pollution" [6].

### Where they differ most
Both candidates say Iowa's water needs to improve. Both oppose the federal beef-import plan, want more cattle on pasture and want more farmland owned by the Iowans who farm it [2][3]. They split on method. Naig would scale up voluntary, cost-shared practices and fund treatment plants. Jones would add mandatory limits or taxes on nutrient inputs and pay farmers to leave the corn-soybean system [5][10]. They also split on ethanol. Naig treats it as a core value-added market and pushes E15. Jones wants the state to plan for a future without corn ethanol [3].

### Context for Polk County voters: Des Moines-area nitrate
- Central Iowa Water Works (CIWW) serves about 600,000 people, one in five Iowans. On June 8, 2026, it imposed a mandatory lawn-watering ban for the second summer in a row. Nitrate in Des Moines Water Works' infiltration gallery reached 12.3 mg/L, against a federal limit of 10, and the Fleur Drive nitrate-removal plant had run 148 days in 2026 by early June, at up to $16,000 a day. CIWW said treated water still met all standards [17].
- Farm to Faucet sends $25 million to CIWW nitrate-removal capacity and targets IDALS conservation money at the 22-county watershed upstream [7]. Jones's platform calls the metro "held hostage by agribusiness and a few thousand upstream polluters" [10]. Naig says upstream and downstream should work "in partnership" [5].

### The nitrate-and-cancer debate
- Iowa has the second-highest cancer incidence in the U.S. and is one of three states with rising rates [18]. A March 2026 report from the Iowa Environmental Council and the Harkin Institute (advocacy and policy groups) links Iowa's cancer rates to environmental risk factors including nitrate, pesticides, PFAS and radon [25][18].
- The University of Iowa's *Cancer in Iowa 2026* report, from the state registry, cites Agricultural Health Study findings. Long-term drinking-water nitrate above 10 mg/L was associated with higher risk of prostate cancer, particularly aggressive disease. An ovarian-cancer association was not statistically significant. The report says "further investigation is needed" [19].
- AP reports that some studies find a nitrate-cancer link but it "remains an area of debate"; EPA does not classify nitrate as a carcinogen, and new University of Iowa research attributes most, but not all, of Iowa's above-normal rates for five cancers to factors such as smoking and binge drinking, with environmental factors to be assessed next [26].
- In the debate Jones said about a million Iowans drink water with nitrate "associated with the whole suite of cancers" and that 7,000 private wells exceed 10 mg/L [4]. No direct statement from Naig on the nitrate-cancer link was found in the sources reviewed.
