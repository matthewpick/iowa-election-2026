# Iowa Treasurer of State — Candidate Profiles

_What the office does._ The treasurer is Iowa's "State's Banker" and chief financial officer. The office deposits and invests state money while keeping enough cash on hand to pay the state's bills, and oversees "billions of dollars in state operating funds" [7]. The treasurer is trustee and custodian of three state pension funds (IPERS, the Peace Officers' Retirement System, and the Judicial Retirement System). The office also runs:
- ISave 529 and the IAdvisor 529 Plan (tax-advantaged education savings; the plan was formerly called College Savings Iowa [3])
- IAble (savings accounts for people with disabilities that don't affect SSI or Medicaid eligibility)
- the Linked Investments for Tomorrow (LIFT) program, which places deposits with banks to make lower-rate loans to small businesses
- unclaimed property, returned through the Great Iowa Treasure Hunt [7][8]

The treasurer is a voting member of the Executive Council, the IPERS Investment Board, the PORS board, the Flood Mitigation Board, the Grain Indemnity Board, the State Appeal Board, the Tobacco Settlement Authority, and other bodies. The treasurer also sits on the Iowa Finance Authority without a vote [7]. The term is four years and the salary is $103,212 [1].

_IPERS governance._ IPERS is the pension system for about 420,000 public employees, with roughly $47 billion in assets [12][3]. The Legislature and governor are the "plan sponsors" and IPERS is the "plan administrator" [11]. The IPERS Investment Board sets investment and actuarial policy. It has seven voting members: three public members with investment experience and three IPERS members, all appointed by the governor and confirmed by the Senate, plus the state treasurer. Four legislators serve on the board without a vote [9]. Under Iowa Code 97B.3, the IPERS chief executive officer is "appointed by the governor subject to confirmation by the senate" for a four-year term [10]. The board does not choose the CEO. A search of the Legislature's records shows 97B.3 was amended in a 2003 extra session [33], but the text of that change was not retrieved, so when governor appointment began is unverified.
- **2026 leadership turnover.** Gov. Reynolds placed CEO Greg Samorajski (her 2020 appointee) on administrative leave March 31, 2026, during a misconduct investigation. He resigned effective May 1 [13][12]. Chief benefits officer Steven Herbert was fired May 7. The Des Moines Register reported that his termination letter cited violations of the sexual harassment and anti-discrimination policies [12].
- **New CEO.** Reynolds named Martin Noven, former head of Maryland's pension system, as CEO starting Aug. 24, 2026, for the rest of the term ending April 30, 2028 [12]. Reynolds said the investigations did not involve IPERS investments [12].
- **DOGE task force.** In 2025 a state-appointed DOGE task force initially recommended exploring a 401(k)-style plan for new hires. Its final report (Oct. 2025) suggested making alternative retirement options part of recurring reviews. Some legislative leaders and Gov. Reynolds said "IPERS isn't going anywhere" [29].
- **The proposed change.** No law changing how the CEO is chosen has been passed. Changing the method is a campaign issue: Norwood proposes that the board, not the governor, pick the CEO. Smith defends the current structure [2][3] (see the profiles below).

_Ballot / candidates:_ Roby Smith (Republican), John Norwood (Democrat) [1]. **Smith is the incumbent** and is seeking a second term [3].

## Roby Smith (Republican)

### At a glance
- **Age:** 49 per Iowa Capital Dispatch [1]; 48 per the Des Moines Register [6]
- **Hometown:** Davenport, where he has lived for 26 years [3]. Born in Wyandotte, Michigan [14].
- **Current role:** Iowa Treasurer of State since Jan. 1, 2023 [7]
- **Education:** B.S. in business administration, Concordia University (Seward, Neb.), 2000 [14][1]
- **Career:**
  - About 10 years as a banker, including at U.S. Bank, and a local business owner [1][3][14]
  - Iowa Senate, 2011–2022 (three terms) [7]

### Background & career
- **Banking and business.** Before holding office, Smith spent "nearly a decade" in banking [7]. He says he runs the office "as a banker, because I am one" [3]. He volunteers teaching financial literacy through Junior Achievement [7].
- **Narrow 2022 win.** IPR reported he won by less than 3 points, becoming the first Republican in the office in 40 years [29].
- **Elections to the Senate.** He won the seat in 2010 by defeating incumbent Sen. David Hartsuch in the GOP primary, 51.9% to 48.1%. He was re-elected in 2014 and 2018, the last time over Democrat Marie Gleason, 52.8% to 47.2% [14].
- **Senate committees.** He chaired the State Government Committee and served on Commerce, Ways and Means, and Rules and Administration [7].
- **2022 treasurer race.** He defeated Democrat Michael Fitzgerald, who had held the office for 40 years, 51.3% to 48.7% [14][2].

### Track record
**Iowa Senate (2011–22)**
- **Election law.** As State Government chair, Smith introduced the 2021 elections bill (SSB 1199) along with Rep. Bobby Kaufmann's identical House bill [25]. The bill:
  - shortened early and mail voting from 29 to 18 days
  - barred auditors from sending absentee request forms unprompted
  - limited ballot drop boxes
  - created criminal penalties for election officials who don't follow state directives [25]
  Smith said the bill created uniform procedures and that a "handful of auditors" had caused "unnecessary confusion and wasteful spending of tax dollars in 2020" [25]. The League of Women Voters, AARP Iowa, and the county auditors' association opposed it [25].
- **Taxes and 529s.** He served on Ways and Means [7]. His 2021 campaign site says he "passed a key reform to make it easier for parents to contribute" to 529 plans [16]. He says he would take a more active role supporting tax cuts if elected (2022 debate summary) [26]. His specific role in the 2018 and 2022 income-tax laws could not be verified (see Open questions).
- **Attendance.** Smith says he never missed a floor vote in 12 years [3][16].

**As treasurer (2023–present)**, all figures self-reported in the Oct. 2026 Iowa Press interview unless noted [3]:
- **Interest earnings.** He says the office actively manages about $10 billion in idle state funds, using laddered bonds, callable bonds and corporate paper. He says this earns more than $1 million a day in interest and more than $1.5 billion in three and a half years, compared with about $100,000 a day in his predecessor's last four years. He says no investment has lost money. KCCI reported that the $1 million-a-day figure comes from a budget presentation to the Legislative Services Agency based on Clearwater Analytics data [4].
- **Deposits in Iowa banks.** He says these grew from about $150 million to more than $500 million. He says he expanded the LIFT program and worked with Sen. Reichman on a bill adding veterans to it.
- **ISave 529.** He rebranded the plan to reach students headed for the trades, and says fees were cut by almost 20%. He says he backed a law raising the per-beneficiary state tax deduction from $3,800 to $6,100. He reports record assets of $7.25 billion. Official ISave 529 releases confirm the $6,100 deduction for 2026, a record above $7 billion in April 2026, and a drop in the plan's only fee from 0.17% to 0.16% effective July 1, 2026 [31].
- **Unclaimed property.** He reports a record $38 million returned last year from 53,000 claims, and more than $100 million during his term. He created "Money Match," which mails unclaimed funds to owners without a claim; the average check last year was $715 [3][4].
- **China investments.** Under Iowa Code Chapter 12K (SF 418), his office filed the required FY2024 reports for the state operating fund, the Judicial Retirement System and PORS. All three reported no direct or indirect holdings in prohibited Chinese companies, so no divestment was needed [20].
- **ESG.**
  - The Register reported that Smith supported Gov. Reynolds' 2023 anti-ESG bill (SSB 1094) [23]. That bill and a 2024 Reynolds bill did not become law [22]. IPERS' then-CEO testified that IPERS already invests only in members' interests [21].
  - Smith appears among state financial officers who signed an August 2023 letter to BlackRock's directors objecting to its ESG commitments [24].
  - In 2022, asked about state business with BlackRock, he said he would defer to lawmakers to set investment policy [14].
  - His 2022 campaign pledged to oppose the Israel boycott (BDS) movement and to divest from "terrorist nations" such as Iran [16].

### What they're running on
Smith's campaign says he is "Protecting YOUR Tax Dollars" [17]. He is running on his record in office, which he calls a "scoreboard business" [3][4]. Ballotpedia lists his 2026 themes as protecting taxpayer dollars, modernizing the office, Treasure Hunt and Money Match returns, lower ISave 529 fees, and IAble [14]. He says he wants to grow Money Match [4] and has visited all 99 counties [3]. His campaign site's "Issues" page still shows 2021–22 content: financial literacy, 529 and IAble outreach, financial privacy, Israel, and divestment [16].

### Positions on key issues
- **IPERS governance.** He would keep governor appointment of the CEO. He says the structure has worked for more than 20 years under both parties and that IPERS has gone from about 80% funded in 2011 to 94.5%. He says no legislator or executive is pushing for changes [3].
- **Keeping money in Iowa banks.** He points to the growth in Iowa bank deposits and LIFT as evidence he is already doing this [3].

### Endorsements & funding
- **Endorsements:** Iowa Association of Business and Industry ("Friend of Iowa Business," 2026); NFIB [14]
- **Fundraising:** $119,731 raised and $21,707 spent, Jan. 1, 2025 – July 14, 2026 [14]

### Criticisms & responses
- **Remote office.** Norwood says Smith tries to do the job "from a remote office in Davenport" and is "a no show in Des Moines" [3][2]. Smith responds that he has never missed a committee meeting or vote, has driven more than 150,000 miles, and that most officials live outside Des Moines [3].
- **Record numbers.** Norwood says the records reflect federal COVID-era deposits and higher interest rates, not "wizardry" [3]. Smith responds that rates are lower now than when he took office, and that the gains come from active management [3].
- **Kennel case (2021).** In 2021, Iowa Capital Dispatch reported that then-Sen. Smith intervened with state agriculture regulators for a Davenport kennel owner. The owner and his mother said Smith warned that an inspector looking into windows "could get shot" because "Iowa is a stand-your-ground state" [27]. Smith declined to comment to the reporter [27].

## John Norwood (Democrat)

### At a glance
- **Age:** 62 [1]
- **Hometown:** West Des Moines, for more than 20 years. Born in Boston [19][15].
- **Current role:** Business advisor, Goldman Sachs 10,000 Small Businesses program (since 2019); founder of TBL Ventures [1][19]
- **Education:** B.A., Williams College (1987); MBA in finance and Master of Environmental Management, Yale (1993) [19][15]
- **Career:**
  - 30+ years in strategy, finance, and governance consulting [19]
  - Polk County Soil & Water Conservation commissioner, 2018–22 [5][15]
  - 2022 Democratic nominee for Secretary of Agriculture [1]

### Background & career
- **Consulting.** Norwood's campaign says he has advised business leaders, municipalities, agricultural cooperatives, state water agencies, and nonprofits on capital allocation, risk, and compliance, including managing "multimillion-dollar portfolios" (self-reported) [19].
- **CFA Society.** He is a long-standing member of the CFA Society of Iowa [19].
- **Green Acres Milling.** He says his company helped launch Green Acres Milling, a farmer-owned oat mill in southern Minnesota [2].
- **Public service.** He cites 20 years on boards and commissions [5].
- **TBL Ventures.** Yale's Center for Business and the Environment describes it as his "boutique management consultancy" and him as a "thirty year entrepreneurial business leader" (self-supplied bio) [30]. Company size and client list were not found.

### Track record
Norwood has not held statewide or legislative office, so his record is professional.
- **Small-business advising.** He says he has personally advised 80 of the roughly 430 Iowa businesses that have gone through Goldman Sachs 10,000 Small Businesses. He says those businesses together employ more than 5,000 people (self-reported) [3]. He has held outreach events for the program in Le Mars, Spencer, and Sioux City [5].
- **Soil and water commissioner.** He was elected Polk County Soil & Water Conservation commissioner in 2018 and served through 2022 [15][5]. His LinkedIn profile lists him as vice chair, Nov. 2018 – Dec. 2022 [32] (self-reported). His Yale bio says he advised on regenerative agriculture and "visioned" the "Batch and Build" edge-of-field program now being scaled across Iowa [30] (self-reported; not independently confirmed). No public votes from that service were found.
- **2022 Secretary of Agriculture race.** He lost to Mike Naig, 38.8% to 61.1% [15]. In that race he:
  - opposed using eminent domain for carbon dioxide pipelines
  - called car fuel "a declining market" for ethanol, proposing it be redirected to aviation, rail, shipping, and long-haul trucking
  - said Naig's water-quality efforts were too slow, citing an estimated 30% rise in nitrate [28]
- **2026 primary.** He won the Democratic nomination with 99.8% [15].

### What they're running on
These are self-reported from his campaign site, Ballotpedia survey, and appearances.
- **IPERS.** Protecting IPERS is his stated "#1 priority" [18]. He launched his campaign in early 2026 saying IPERS is "foundational to the Iowa economy" and wants to broaden retirement-savings access, citing a waitress or small business worker [29]. He opposes privatization or a 401(k)-style switch. He cites the governor's DOGE task force report as a privatization threat [18][3].
- **Transparency and the budget.** He wants a "visible, trusted" treasurer who warns lawmakers about Iowa's "$1+ billion structural budget deficit" [18][15]. He wants to protect the state's AAA bond rating [15][5].
- **Investing in Iowa.** He would move more short-term state deposits, including about $2.5 billion in pooled funds paid to schools and local governments, into Iowa banks and credit unions so they can lend locally [2][3]. He would "modernize" and "right size" LIFT and use it to help diversify agriculture [3][4].
- **Agriculture.** He wants treasury help financing "third crops" such as oats and regional farmer-led processing, which he ties to water quality [2][3].
- **ISave 529.** He would market it to students headed for the trades or business ownership, widen participation across income levels, and improve customer service [3][4][18].
- **Other priorities he named at the State Fair:** "putting the brakes" on private-school Education Savings Accounts, and union labor on state projects [2].

### Positions on key issues
- **IPERS governance.** The board, not the governor, should hire and fire the CEO. The risk officer should report to the board [2][3]. He wanted an independent investigation and an interim CEO after the 2026 departures [2]. He cites a pending whistleblower lawsuit in Polk County that he says involves the firing of a risk officer and allegations about performance bonuses [3].
- **Education Savings Accounts.** He says they cost $330 million and would add an income test [4][5].
- **Flat tax.** He says the 3.8% flat tax cost "over $1 billion" in revenue [4].
- **Steel-mill incentives.** He calls for "very careful scrutiny" of the developer and says keeping deposits in Iowa would cost far less [3].
- **Recovering harassment settlements.** He supports making perpetrators pay [3].
- **Residency.** He says the treasurer should live in the Des Moines area [2][3].

### Endorsements & funding
- **Endorsements:** Iowa Federation of Labor, AFL-CIO; International Union of Operating Engineers Local 150 [15]
- **Fundraising:** $59,645 raised and $16,001 spent, Jan. 1, 2025 – July 14, 2026 [15]

### Criticisms & responses
- **IPERS change.** Smith says Norwood is "the only person that wants to make changes to IPERS" and that the current structure has produced strong funding [3]. Norwood responds that the 2026 departures and the whistleblower suit show "governance, structural issues" needing more board oversight [3].
- **Banking experience.** Smith says he is "the only one that has the banking background" [3]. Norwood responds that his finance career and small-business advising mean he knows "quite a bit about banking" [3].
- **DOGE/privatization claim.** Norwood says Republicans want to move new hires to a 401(k)-style plan [18]. The task force's final report only suggested alternatives be part of recurring reviews, and Reynolds and legislative leaders said "IPERS isn't going anywhere" [29].
- No other published criticism of Norwood was found.

### Where they differ most
- **IPERS leadership.** The sharpest policy difference is who picks the IPERS CEO. Smith would keep the governor-appointed model. Norwood would give the job to the board and make oversight staff report to the board.
- **What the office is for.** Smith presents the office as a money manager judged by its returns. Norwood presents it as a public voice on the state budget and on where state money is deposited.
- **Budget and tax policy.** Norwood openly criticizes the flat tax and Education Savings Accounts. Smith did not address them in the coverage reviewed.
- **Location.** They disagree on whether the treasurer needs to be based in Des Moines.
