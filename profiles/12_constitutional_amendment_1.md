# Constitutional Amendment 1 — Two-Thirds Vote for Income Tax Increases

_What it is._ A legislatively referred amendment that would add a new Article XIII to the Iowa Constitution. It appears on the back of the Clive 4 ballot, in column two, under "Constitutional Amendment 1" [1][2]. A "yes" vote adds the requirement. A "no" vote leaves the current rule, a simple majority in each chamber [3][4].

## What the ballot says
The ballot summary: "Provides that any bill to increase the individual income tax rate or the corporate income tax rate, or the rate of any other type of tax based upon income or legal and special reserves, shall require the affirmative votes of at least two-thirds of the members elected to each house of the legislature for passage. Further, the two-thirds majority vote requirement applies to the passage of a bill to establish a new tax on any type of income or legal and special reserves imposed by the state. The requirement excludes taxes imposed at the option of a local government. A lawsuit challenging the proper enactment of a bill under this section must be filed no later than one year following the enactment." [1][2]

The full text adds that "if such a lawsuit is not filed within the one-year limit, the bill shall be considered properly enacted," that each covered bill "must include a separate provision describing the requirements for enactment," and that "the general assembly shall enact laws to implement this section" [1][2].

## What it would and would not change
- **Votes needed.** The threshold counts members elected, not members present. That is 67 of 100 House members and 34 of 50 senators, up from 51 and 26 [3][4].
- **Covered:** increases in the individual or corporate income tax rate, increases in the rate of "any other type of tax based upon income or legal and special reserves," and new state taxes on those categories [1][5]. Ballotpedia says "legal and special reserves" are funds that Iowa credit unions must maintain [5].
- **Not covered:** lowering income tax rates, raising sales or property tax rates, and taxes imposed at a local government's option [1][4].
- **Does not change any tax by itself.** KTIV and Iowa Public Radio both say it would not raise or lower anyone's taxes [3][4].

## Current law
- **Individual income tax:** a flat 3.8% under Senate File 2442 (May 2024), applying to all taxable income in 2026 [6].
- **Corporate income tax:** from January 1, 2024, 5.5% on income up to $100,000 and 7.1% above that. A 2022 law (HF 2317) provides for further automatic reductions if corporate receipts exceed a threshold, with a floor of 5.5% [7].
- **Sales tax:** 6%, last raised in 2008 [4].

## How it reached the ballot
Iowa amendments need identical resolutions from two successive General Assemblies. No governor signature is required, and a simple majority of voters decides [3][8].

All "yes" votes came from Republicans and all "no" votes from Democrats, per Ballotpedia's party breakdown [5]. At the May 2, 2026 House debate, amendments to the text were offered and defeated 21–57 or 22–56 on procedural and adoption votes [10]. Changing the text would have restarted the process [8].

## Arguments for (self-described, attributed)
- **Iowans for Tax Relief (ITR), which runs the "Make Tax Hikes Harder" campaign:** a supermajority "forces consensus." It says tax increases would need "a broader coalition of elected officials" and that the rule works "as an indirect spending restraint" [5]. ITR president Chris Hagenow said the amendment guards against "the political pendulum" and called warnings of spending cuts "scare tactics." He rejected the claim that limiting one tax means raising another [4].
- **ITR policy director John Hendrickson:** the amendment "does not require spending cuts, reductions in school funding, or increases in sales or other state taxes" [5].
- **Americans for Prosperity and Americans for Tax Reform:** AFP's Tyler Raygor called it "a prudent safeguard against reckless spending." ATR pointed to Arizona's supermajority rule [5].
- **Rep. John Wills (R):** said the current budget shortfall "was planned" and that the resolution lets Iowans decide [11].
- **Editorial board (The Black Chronicle):** supports the measure [5].
- **Listed supporters (Ballotpedia):** Republican Party of Iowa; Sens. Dan Dawson and Rocky De Witt; Reps. Dan Gehlbach, Bobby Kaufmann and John Wills; Americans for Prosperity; Americans for Tax Reform; Iowa Association of Business and Industry; Iowa Credit Union League; National Federation of Independent Business; and others [5].

## Arguments against (self-described, attributed)
- **Common Good Iowa, which leads the "Fair Taxes for Iowa's Future" coalition:** executive director Anne Discher said the measure protects "the two taxes that can be structured in a way that makes sure that the wealthiest folks... and the most profitable corporations pay what they owe." She said lawmakers could turn to uncovered taxes, such as the sales tax, or to cuts [4].
- **League of Women Voters of Iowa (Linda Hagedorn):** surpluses are temporary, but "Iowa's Constitution... is meant to endure" [5].
- **Rep. David Jacoby (D):** "I do not believe in minority rules"; he said it would "tie the hands of the Legislature" [5].
- **Editorial boards:** The Gazette wrote that it "would allow the minority to rule." The Des Moines Register wrote that it would "rig the Iowa Constitution" in favor of a minority [5].
- **Listed opponents (Ballotpedia):** Iowa Democratic Party; Sen. Tony Bisignano and Rep. David Jacoby; Iowa State Education Association; Iowa Federation of Labor (AFL-CIO); AFSCME Council 61; Iowa Farmers Union; Iowa Catholic Conference; Progress Iowa; and others [5][12].

## Points each side disputes
- **Budget context.** Opponents cite a projected shortfall of more than $1 billion and use of the Taxpayer Relief Fund [4][11]. Supporters, including Wills, say the shortfall was planned [11]. Democrats, including Auditor Rob Sand, argue the use of reserves reflects a structural imbalance from the tax cuts; Republicans disagree [11].
- **Effect on other taxes.** Discher says the amendment makes sales-tax increases or spending cuts more likely. Hagenow rejects that [4].
- **Initiative process.** Discher noted that 10 of the 15 states with such requirements also have a citizen-initiative process, which Iowa lacks [4].

## Other states and past Iowa votes
- NCSL counted 15 states with a supermajority requirement to raise income taxes as of April 2025. Four require 3/5, nine require 2/3 and two require 3/4. In 13 of the 15, the requirement covers all taxes, not only income taxes [5]. ITR counts 17 states with a similar rule [5]; the sources don't explain the difference.
- The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, a progressive think tank, found that states with and without such requirements have similar levels of taxation [4].
- Since 2000, Ballotpedia counts six statewide votes on supermajority tax requirements. Washington (2007, 2010, 2012) and Florida (2018) approved them. Michigan (2012) and Louisiana (2025) rejected them [5].
- In June 1999, Iowa voters rejected a three-fifths requirement covering income and sales taxes, with 52.0% voting no. That vote was in a special election with about 400,000 voters [5][8].

## Campaign money
Ballotpedia's figures run through July 19, 2026, plus ITR's later independent-expenditure filing. No committee was registered in support. ITR reported $971,986.94 in independent expenditures supporting Amendment 1 as of October 2026. Fair Taxes for Iowa's Future and Working Iowans for Fair Taxes registered in opposition. Opposition committees had raised $23,957.64 and spent $0 as of July [5]. The October 19 reports were not yet processed there [5].

## Open questions / couldn't verify
- **Legislative Services Agency fiscal note:** I did not find one. Searches returned only social-media posts, which are not usable sources.
- **Current campaign totals:** I did not pull the Iowa Ethics and Campaign Disclosure Board filings from October 19 or later. The figures above may be out of date.
- **Base changes:** The text speaks of rate increases and new taxes. None of the sources I read says how changes to the tax base, such as ending a deduction, would be classified.
- **Court interpretation:** The one-year lawsuit window and the "separate provision" rule have not been tested in court. No source I read addresses them.
- **Corporate rates after 2024:** The Department of Revenue page I read shows rates only through the 2024 schedule. HF 2317's triggers may have changed 2025–26 rates.
- **Supporter list entry:** Ballotpedia lists "Iowa State Treasurer's Office" as a supporter, but I found no primary statement from that office.
