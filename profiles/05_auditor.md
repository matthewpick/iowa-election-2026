# Iowa Auditor of State — Candidate Profiles

_What the office does._ The Auditor of State is a constitutional officer elected to a four-year term. The auditor must audit the books and accounts of every state department each year, audits the state's Annual Comprehensive Financial Report and Single Audit, is responsible for audits of counties, cities, school districts and other local governments, and sets guidelines for private CPA firms that audit local governments. The auditor also sits on the State Executive Council and the State Appeal Board [1]. The 2026 salary is $103,212 [2].

_Background: the 2023 audit-access law (Senate File 478)._ The law limits the auditor's access to certain sensitive records, including medical and student records. Exceptions apply when the records are needed to meet auditing standards or state or federal requirements, or when theft or embezzlement is suspected [4]. It also changed how records disputes are settled. A dispute between the auditor and an executive-branch office or agency now goes to a three-member arbitration panel instead of a court. Each side appoints one member, the governor appoints the third, and the panel's decision is final [3][4]. The Senate passed the final version 33–16 on April 26, 2023, and Gov. Kim Reynolds signed it [3][7]. Its floor manager, Sen. Mike Bousselot (R), pointed to Iowa Supreme Court rulings on Auditor Rob Sand's subpoenas and said "the watchdog still has teeth" while Iowans' private data is protected [3]. Opponents included the National State Auditors Association, the Institute of Internal Auditors and former U.S. Comptroller General David Walker. They wrote to lawmakers warning that the law could put federal funds at risk. Sand called it "the greatest pro-corruption bill" in Iowa history [3].

_Ballot / candidates:_ Chris Cournoyer (R), Taylor Wettach (D) [2]. **Open seat.** Incumbent Rob Sand (D) is running for governor [2][5].

## Chris Cournoyer (Republican)

### At a glance
- **Age / home:** 55; LeClaire (Scott County) [2][5]. Born in Dallas, Texas, per Ballotpedia [14].
- **Current role:** Lieutenant Governor of Iowa. Gov. Reynolds appointed her on Dec. 16, 2024, to replace Adam Gregg. Her term ends in January 2027 [14][10].
- **Education:** B.A. in computer science, University of Texas at Austin, 1992 [14].
- **Career:** Senior consultant in Andersen Consulting's (now Accenture) technology division. Then 20+ years running her own website design and development business [2][9].
- **Public service:** Pleasant Valley School Board, 2013–19, including two years as president. Scott County reserve sheriff's deputy. Iowa Senate, 2019–24 (District 49, then District 35) [2][14].

### Background & career
Cournoyer told KCCI she had not planned to run for office until the 2012 Sandy Hook school shooting [8]. In the Senate she chaired the Technology Committee and the Education Budget subcommittee and was vice chair of State Government [9][14]. Reynolds appointed her lieutenant governor in December 2024. Cournoyer resigned her Senate seat and announced her run for auditor in May 2025 [10][14]. In her Ballotpedia survey she lists her current roles as co-chair of the Governor's STEM Council, co-chair of Empower Rural Iowa, and chair of Iowa's America 250 initiative (self-reported) [14]. She says she has visited all 99 counties as lieutenant governor [6]. She won the June 2 Republican primary over Iowa County Supervisor Abigail Maas, 54.0% to 45.7% [14].

### Track record
- **SF 478 (2023):** Voted yes. She has defended the vote throughout the campaign [5][6][7].
- **Government realignment (2023):** The law cut cabinet-level agencies from 37 to 16. Cournoyer describes herself as one of its leaders [10]. Her campaign credits realignment and IT consolidation with "over $200 million" in savings (self-reported) [13][5].
- **Technology and cybersecurity bills she floor-managed (2023–24)** [15]:
  - SF 262, consumer data privacy. It passed unanimously and took effect Jan. 1, 2025 [15][17][18]. VitalLaw identified her as the bill's sponsor and Technology Committee chair [17].
  - SF 203, ransomware penalties. It was signed by the governor on May 11, 2023 [24].
  - SF 402, a cybersecurity simulation training center at Iowa State. The bill did not become law: it was introduced Feb. 23, 2023, and a Senate subcommittee recommended passage Mar. 22, 2023, but the history shows no further action [25].
  - HF 632, school SAVE-fund money for cybersecurity.
  - SF 2186 and HF 2240, harassment using altered ("deepfake") images. HF 2240 took effect July 1, 2024.
- **Education bills she floor-managed** [15]:
  - SF 250, the computer science professional-development fund (took effect July 1, 2023).
  - SF 2332, computer science graduation requirements [15][16]. The bill did not become law: it was introduced and approved by committee on Feb. 14, 2024, and the history shows no later action [26].
  - SF 398, school robotics activities.
  - SF 2385, which cut state boards and commissions (took effect 2024–25).
- **DOGE task force:** Reynolds created the Iowa DOGE Task Force by executive order on Feb. 10, 2025. It was chaired by Emily Schmitt of Sukup Manufacturing and staffed by 15–20 business leaders. Its goals were return on taxpayer investment, workforce programs, and technology including AI [11]. The final report was submitted Sept. 29, 2025, and released Oct. 21, 2025 [12]. The final report's roster lists Chair Emily Schmitt, 12 other business and local-government members, and two ex-officio legislators (Rep. Hans Wilz and Sen. Dan Dawson). Cournoyer is not named in it [27]. The report does recommend, in the local-government section, that Iowa consider a shared small-city accounting system modeled on Minnesota's, which the state auditor could use to aggregate local data (pp. 66–67, 71) [27]. Cournoyer's stated role is prospective: she pledges to make the auditor's office "a home for recommendations emerging from the Governor's DOGE Task Force" and to work with state and local governments to carry them out (self-reported) [10][13][14].

### What they're running on
These are self-reported priorities [10][13][14]:
- Modernize the office with financial-auditing software and AI to speed up audits. She says human CPAs must remain "in the loop" [4][5].
- Spread audit staff across the state, and do more in-person audits instead of desk audits [6][14].
- Create a grant program and offer technical help so local governments can adopt transparency technology, including public finance dashboards. She ties the savings to property-tax relief [6][14].
- Promote the state's transparency portal [10].
- "Take the politics out of the office." She says she would pursue anyone who steals from taxpayers regardless of party [14].
- Continue or expand Sand's Public Innovations and Efficiencies (PIE) program [4][5].

### Positions on key issues
- **SF 478:** Supports it. She says the law sets "guardrails" that protect medical and student records and that arbitration avoids costly lawsuits between agencies [4][6].
- **Education Savings Accounts (ESAs):** She has "no problem" auditing the program, whether the audit is done by the auditor or a third party. She cites existing controls and accreditation [5][6].
- **Prison time for stealing $10,000+ in public money:** Supports it, plus restitution [6].
- **Requiring school districts to use the auditor:** Opposes a mandate, citing the office's limited capacity [6].
- **State agencies hiring private auditors:** Open to it, with guidelines and the auditor's oversight [6].
- **Office budget:** She would assess needs and use her ties with legislators to make the case for an increase "if that's necessary" [6].
- **Public commentary:** "The opinions of the auditor should not be made public" [8]. She would consider evaluating the legislative budget at the end of session [6].

### Endorsements & funding
- **Endorsements (self-reported):** Gov. Reynolds, U.S. Sen. Joni Ernst, U.S. Rep. Randy Feenstra, state Sen. Jack Whitver, and other state legislators [13][14].
- **Funding:** $244,047 raised and $237,176 spent, Jan. 1, 2025 – July 14, 2026 (Ballotpedia/Transparency USA) [14]. In the October report covering mid-July to mid-October she raised $201,970 and reported $189,603 cash on hand, per Des Moines Register and Iowa Capital Dispatch coverage [28][29].
- **Party support:** The Iowa GOP's Facebook page has posted a pro-Cournoyer message and an anti-Wettach video (see below) [30].

### Criticisms & responses
- **On SF 478:** Wettach says her vote "made it more difficult for this office to protect folks' tax money" and calls it a main reason he entered the race [4][6]. **Response:** Cournoyer says the auditor "is not entitled to your medical records" unless they are tied to the audit. She says the office has "done hundreds of audits since then" [6].
- **On ties to the governor:** Wettach's campaign site says Reynolds is trying to "anoint" Cournoyer, making her a "fox guarding the hen house" (campaign) [19]. **Response:** No direct reply was found. Cournoyer says she would hold wrongdoers accountable regardless of party, "even if it will not make me popular with some members of my own party" (self-reported) [14].
- **On efficiency:** Wettach says efficiency "can't be a code word for let's gut the office" [5]. No direct response was found.
- **Qualifications:** She is not a CPA. Iowa Press noted that Republicans criticized Sand for the same thing in 2018 [6]. **Response:** She points to her leadership, technology and budget experience and says she would rely on the office's CPAs [6][9].

## Taylor Wettach (Democrat)

### At a glance
- **Age / home:** 35; Muscatine. Seventh-generation Iowan [2][9].
- **Current role:** Attorney. He has not held elected office [5].
- **Education:** Georgetown University School of Foreign Service; New York University School of Law [9].
- **Career:** Early work on international economic and policy issues. About four years as an associate at Simpson Thacher & Bartlett, working on capital markets, international trade, regulatory and national security matters [9][21].
- **Other:** Eagle Scout [6]. His father was a family doctor and his mother a preschool teacher (self-reported) [18].

### Background & career
Wettach grew up in Muscatine and attended public schools (self-reported) [18]. At Simpson Thacher, where KCCI reports he worked in Washington, D.C. [8], he says he reviewed "multibillion-dollar financial issues" and worked with auditors and accountants [4][20]. His campaign says he was recruited to a federal job requiring a top-level security clearance, a position later "impacted by" the federal DOGE initiative (self-reported; details not independently confirmed) [18][7].

**Departure from Simpson Thacher:** The firm was one of several reported to have agreed to roughly $125 million in free legal work for Trump administration priorities to avoid executive orders aimed at law firms [2]. Wettach resigned in May 2025 [21]. He says he quit because the firm "bowed to political pressure" and calls it "not a left or right sort of thing; it's about right and wrong" [5][8]. In July 2025 he launched a run for Congress in Iowa's 1st District [21]. In January 2026 he left that race to run for auditor [8][23]. He announced the congressional run on July 8, 2025, in a campaign video [32]. When he switched races in January 2026, the Register reported that he criticized Cournoyer for voting for the 2023 law [23]. At a Jan. 31 voter event reported by the Newton Daily News, he said Iowans want an auditor with "the humility to listen and the courage to say what needs to be said" (excerpt) [33]. He was unopposed in the June 2 Democratic primary (99.8%, 176,429 votes) [22].

**Self-described values:** His campaign site says he learned "values the Iowa way," that his father was a family doctor who cared for people "no matter… how they voted," and that "accountability isn't a Democratic value or a Republican value – it's an Iowa value" (campaign, self-reported) [18][19]. On Iowa Press he cited his Eagle Scout background and said the auditor must "call a spade a spade" [6].

### Track record
Wettach has no voting or government record. His record is professional, as described above. Public detail on specific matters he handled is limited, apart from practice areas reported by the Iowa Society of CPAs (ISCPA) and Law.com [9][21]. He spoke to the ISCPA Legislation Committee in August 2026 [9]. He appeared at the Des Moines Register Political Soapbox at the State Fair; Cournoyer did not take part [20]. He appeared jointly with Cournoyer on Iowa Press, recorded Oct. 23, 2026 [6].

### What they're running on
These are self-reported priorities [19][20]:
- **Repeal or reverse SF 478**, whoever is governor: "It shouldn't matter if the governor is a Democrat or if the governor is a Republican" [20]. He would restore judicial review of records disputes [5].
- **Protect the office's independence.** He calls this his top goal [7]. He would keep Sand's practice of having Democratic, Republican and independent deputies review office decisions [5].
- **Continue Sand's PIE program,** and add technology, AI and targeted data analysis while keeping staff resources [4][9].
- **Help rural areas and small towns up front.** He compares it to "primary care versus… expensive treatment" [20][6].
- **Make ESAs his first audit priority,** and seek more access to records from participating private schools and program administrators [20][5].
- **Look more closely at** corporate tax incentives, uncollected fines, wage theft and worker misclassification [5].

### Positions on key issues
- **SF 478:** Opposes it. He says the arbitration panel lets audited agencies influence the outcome, "like letting an accused… determine who the judge or the jury is going to be" [6].
- **ESAs:** Families should be free to choose private schools, but "if you're taking public money, you should be held to the same standards." He says the program has "almost no oversight" [5][7].
- **Prison time for stealing $10,000+:** Supports it [6].
- **State agencies hiring private auditors:** Skeptical. "What is the point of having an auditor if you're not going to let the auditor do its job?" [6]
- **Office budget:** He says the office has been "strangled" by underfunding and limits on its powers [6].
- **Real-time transaction dashboard:** Supports it, so long as the auditor can be trusted to make the information clear "without political favor" [6].
- **ESA costs:** He cited program costs rising from $130 million in the first year to $330 million "this past year" (his figures; not verified here) [6].
- **Where he would live / work:** Undecided; likely to split time between Muscatine and Des Moines. He praised Sand's annual 99-county meeting series [6].
- **Office funding and staffing:** Says the legislature has not listened to the office's requests. He cites a Legislative Services Agency finding, put to the candidates by the Iowa Press panel, that the office's budget has not increased in eight years [6].
- **School-district audits (Iowa City):** He declined to comment on a specific case but said the office needs "teeth" and resources [6].

### Endorsements & funding
- **Endorsements:** Iowa Federation of Labor, AFL-CIO; International Union of Operating Engineers Local 150 (per Ballotpedia) [22].
- **Funding:** $704,530 raised and $113,472 spent, Jan. 1, 2025 – July 14, 2026 (Ballotpedia/Transparency USA) [22]. In the October report covering mid-July to mid-October he raised $778,147 and reported $683,193 cash on hand [28][29]. Some of his earlier receipts may date from his congressional campaign; that was not checked.

### Criticisms & responses
- **Cournoyer's critique of the incumbent party's use of the office:** She says Sand "used this office to run for higher office" and left staff and offices empty (self-reported; Iowa Press) [14][6]. Wettach praised the Sand-era office while saying the office was "strangled" by funding and legal limits [6][20].
- **Qualifications:** He is not a CPA and has never held office [5][6]. **Response:** He says his legal work reviewing complex transactions complements the CPAs on staff and would help when audits turn up legal issues [9].
- **Continuity with Sand:** Cournoyer accuses Sand of politicizing the office, including through his criticism of the ESA program [5]. Wettach has praised Sand's tenure and pledged to continue his work [20]. **Response:** Wettach pledged to scrutinize a Democratic administration as aggressively as a Republican one [5]. He told CPAs one advantage was simply "not being Rob Sand" [9].
- **Residency / outsider framing (Iowa GOP):** A video on the Iowa GOP's Facebook page is titled "East Coast liberal lawyer Taylor Wettach moved in with parents to run for office" and portrays him as too liberal for the race [30]. I could only see the search excerpt, not the video. **Response:** None found for this video. Wettach describes himself as born and raised in Muscatine and a seventh-generation Iowan, and on The Iowa Podcast said he is a "finance attorney," not a "career politician" (excerpt only) [18][31].
- **Cournoyer's own criticism of Wettach:** She has not been quoted attacking him personally. Her criticism is aimed at Sand and "political activist" auditing (see above) [5][10].

### Where they differ most
The clearest split is over SF 478 and over who should settle records disputes between the auditor and executive agencies. Cournoyer voted for the arbitration panel and says it protects Iowans' medical and student data and keeps agencies out of costly lawsuits. Wettach would seek to return those disputes to judges, arguing that the current panel favors the agencies being audited.

Both describe the auditor as an "independent watchdog," but they mean different things. For Cournoyer, independence means an auditor who relies on facts and does not publicly voice opinions about the programs under audit. She contrasts that with Sand's public criticism of ESAs. For Wettach, independence means protecting the office's powers from the governor and legislature, whichever party holds them. His campaign argues that Cournoyer's current role in the Reynolds administration is part of the problem. She says she would pursue wrongdoing regardless of party.

They also differ on emphasis. Cournoyer stresses efficiency, carrying out DOGE recommendations, and technology. Wettach stresses the office's authority and resources, more scrutiny of ESAs, and broader areas such as tax incentives and wage theft.
