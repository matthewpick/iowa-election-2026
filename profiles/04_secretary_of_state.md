# Iowa Secretary of State — Candidate Profiles

_What the office does._ The Secretary of State is Iowa's state commissioner of elections. The office supervises election laws and rules, works with all 99 county auditors, runs the statewide voter registration system, trains election officials, and accepts nomination papers from statewide, federal, legislative and some judicial candidates [1]. It also runs the state's business services: business and nonprofit registrations, Uniform Commercial Code and mechanic's lien filings, and notary commissions. It also administers Safe at Home, an address-confidentiality program for survivors of violent crime [1]. The term is 4 years and the salary is $103,212 [3].

_Ballot order / candidates._ Paul D. Pate (Republican) and Ryan Peterman (Democrat) [3]. **Incumbent race:** Pate has held the office since 2015 and is seeking a fifth term [3][5].

---

## Paul D. Pate (Republican)

### At a glance
- **Age:** 68 [3]
- **Hometown:** Cedar Rapids [5]
- **Current role:** Iowa Secretary of State since 2015. He also held the office from 1995 to 1999 [3].
- **Education:** Associate degree, Kirkwood Community College (1978) [3][4]. He earned the CERA election-administrator certification while in office (self-reported) [4].
- **Career:** Owner of Pate Asphalt, a third-generation family business [3][4]. Iowa Senate, 1989–93 [3]. Mayor of Cedar Rapids, 2002–06 [3]. Former president of the Iowa League of Cities, and president of the National Association of Secretaries of State (NASS) in 2019–20 [4].

### Background & career
After serving in the Iowa Senate and a first term as Secretary of State in the 1990s, Pate was mayor of Cedar Rapids from 2002 to 2006 [3]. He returned to the Secretary of State's office in 2015 and won his fourth reelection in 2022 over Democrat Joel Miller, the former Linn County auditor [9]. In his Ballotpedia survey he says election administration "is not partisan" and that the Secretary's job is to "serve as the referee" (self-reported) [4]. Within NASS he has co-chaired the Elections Committee twice and the Business Services Committee once [2].

### Track record
- **Voter ID and election procedures.** His official biography credits him with online voter registration, Iowa's voter ID requirement, post-election audits, and stronger election cybersecurity [2]. The 2017 voter ID law also cut the early-voting window from 40 days to 29. A 2021 law passed by the Legislature, SF 413, cut it to 20 days [13].
- **Business services and victim programs.** He launched Fast Track Filing for business documents in 2018 [1]. His office cites more than 2,000 participants in Safe at Home and about 1,000 members of the Iowa Businesses Against Trafficking coalition [2].
- **2024 challenged-ballot episode.** About two weeks before the Nov. 5, 2024 election, with early voting under way, Pate told county auditors to challenge the ballots of 2,176 registered voters [11][12]. These were people who had told the Iowa DOT or another agency in the previous 12 years that they were not citizens. He said the federal agency USCIS had denied Iowa access to its SAVE citizenship database [12]. The ACLU of Iowa and LULAC sued on behalf of naturalized citizens. On Nov. 3, 2024, U.S. District Judge Stephen Locher declined to block the guidance. He noted that the noncitizen share appeared to be "no more than twelve percent" and that auditors could use their own judgment [12]. In March 2025 Pate reported that 277 people on the list were confirmed noncitizens and that 35 noncitizens had cast ballots that were counted [9][10].
- **SAVE lawsuit and settlement.** Pate and Attorney General Brenna Bird sued the U.S. Department of Homeland Security over SAVE access in December 2024. A Dec. 1, 2025 settlement gives Iowa 20 years of access. In exchange, Iowa shares state driver's license data with the federal government and joins a bulk-upload pilot that includes Social Security numbers. Iowa Capital Dispatch reported that Pate's and Bird's statements did not mention the license data [10]. A 2025 law Pate proposed lets his office contract with outside agencies to verify voter rolls. It also created an "unconfirmed" registration status and allows challenges to a voter's citizenship at the polls [9][10]. Pate says he will issue no noncitizen list in 2026 because Iowa now has SAVE access [7].
- **DOJ voter-data request (2026).** On May 5, 2026, his office announced that Iowa had complied with the Justice Department's request for voter registration data [25]. See "The voter-data dispute" below.
- **Fraud referrals.** Iowa PBS's moderator noted that of the cases Pate referred to the Attorney General that have been prosecuted so far, one has ended in a conviction. Pate said enforcement is up to law enforcement and the Legislature [5].
- **English-only materials.** In 2020 his office confirmed that Iowa law requires English-only voter registration forms [30]. In May 2025 the Iowa Supreme Court reversed a lower-court ruling and rejected LULAC's lawsuit over English-only voting materials [28].
- **Federal election monitors.** On Oct. 28, 2026 he confirmed that DOJ monitors are expected at "a couple locations" in Iowa [15].

### What they're running on
Pate asks voters to judge him on his record: voter ID, election security, and SAVE citizenship checks [3]. His slogan is "Easy to Vote, Hard to Cheat" (campaign, self-reported) [21]. His stated priorities are keeping Iowa top-rated in election administration, "balancing election integrity with voter participation," modernizing business systems, and supporting the Safe at Home and anti-trafficking programs (self-reported) [4]. He describes his approach to election security as "layered": voter ID, paper ballots, public testing of vote-counting machines, post-election audits, poll workers, and cybersecurity [2][5].

### Positions on key issues
- **Early voting window:** He calls the current 20 days "too short" and is working with county auditors from both parties to add five days [5][14].
- **Voter ID:** He calls it "the real gold standard" [5].
- **Signature matching:** He said it "shouldn't be the determining factor" for verifying voters [5].
- **Open primaries:** He said "I don't think it's something that we're ready for today" [5].
- **2026 attestation repeal (HF 2501):** The law eliminated a voter's ability to establish identity through another voter's attestation. It took effect July 1, 2026 [27][29]. No public statement by Pate on it was found. On voter ID generally, he says he "fought hard for" it [5].
- **Non-English ballots:** No 2026 statement found. His office's 2020 confirmation of the English-only registration rule is noted above [30].
- **Approval voting:** He calls it "just another term for ranked choice voting" and does not favor it [5].
- **Election Day holiday:** He opposes it [5].
- **Federal election monitors:** He expects them at "a couple locations" and warns that fear-mongering about them could disenfranchise voters [15].
- **Trump executive order:** He expressed "serious concerns" about the order to compile a federal voter list (April 2026) [6].

### Endorsements & funding
- **Endorsements:** Ballotpedia lists only one 2026 voter-guide recommendation for him, from a small "Sway" voting group, and no major organizations [31]. His campaign site still shows a 2022 release saying every statewide and federal Iowa Republican endorsed him that year [21]. A Republican Party-affiliated Facebook video promotes his campaign (search excerpt only) [32]. The Indiana Secretary of State's committee gave him $5,000 in August, which he later returned [16].
- **Fundraising:** In July 2026 he reported $2,110 raised and about $90,000 cash on hand [17][18]. In the October period he raised about $48,000, and he will have about $36,000 on hand after a refund described below [16].

### Criticisms & responses
- **DOJ data sharing.** Peterman calls the decision "indefensible"; Pate says he followed legal advice. State Sen. Janet Peterson (D) said a March 5 letter from Pate to the DOJ appeared to contradict an earlier staff assurance, and called it "lying by omission." See the dispute section below [6][5][26].
- **2024 noncitizen list.** The ACLU of Iowa said the list consisted "primarily, or entirely, of U.S. citizens" [11]. Peterman calls it a last-minute "purge" [5]. Pate says he had "no choice" because the Biden administration would not share SAVE data. He says he emphasized provisional ballots to avoid putting a "scarlet letter" on anyone [7][5].
- **Ethics complaint.** On Oct. 20, 2026, Lara Plecas of Des Moines, who has backed Peterman on social media, filed an ethics complaint. It said a $5,000 contribution to Pate from the campaign of Indiana Secretary of State Diego Morales violated Iowa Code 68A.301, which bars contributions from other candidates' committees. Peterman called it "blatant corruption." Pate returned the money the next day and said, "We have returned the monies and are amending our financial report" [16]. The Iowa Ethics and Campaign Disclosure Board had not responded to the Register's questions at publication, and no ruling was found [16]. Peterman added that Pate had campaigned with Morales at the State Fair and that "a career politician like Paul Pate who has been in office for nearly 40 years should know better" [16].

## Ryan Peterman (Democrat)

### At a glance
- **Age:** 35 [3]
- **Hometown:** Grew up in Bettendorf. He lives in Davenport [20][5].
- **Current role:** Candidate. This is his first run for elected office [3].
- **Education:** U.S. Naval Academy graduate [3][20]
- **Career:** Navy helicopter pilot for about 10 years, per Iowa Capital Dispatch [3]. Defense/legislative fellow in the U.S. Senate for Sen. Jeanne Shaheen (D-N.H.) [3][9]. Honor education officer at the Naval Academy [3]. His campaign says he served "fifteen years in uniform" (self-reported) [20]; Iowa PBS used the same figure [5]. The two figures may measure different things, such as flying versus total service, but no source reconciles them.
- **Political experience:** None in elected office; this is his first campaign [3]. He says he voted by mail while in the Navy [5].

### Background & career
Peterman says he grew up "in a family full of teachers, veterans, and small business owners" (self-reported) [20]. After graduating from the Naval Academy, he earned his wings as a naval aviator and flew helicopters. He told the N'West Iowa REVIEW he served with a San Diego-based squadron and spent significant time in the Persian Gulf, where he led a division of nearly 30 sailors [18]. The Navy then sent him to the U.S. Senate for about a year as a fellow working on defense and national security policy, in the office of Sen. Jeanne Shaheen [9][18]. His last Navy tour was back at the Naval Academy as honor education and remediation officer, where he taught midshipmen about the honor code [18][3]. He announced his campaign in May 2025 with events in Davenport and Des Moines, saying his experiences give him a foundation to serve "with integrity, transparency, and nonpartisan professionalism" [9]. He says he came home because the part of public life he missed most was being a public servant, and that Iowa had "changed," most notably in public education [18]. His campaign lists him as a Naval aviator, Senate defense fellow, and Naval Academy mentor (self-reported) [19]. His campaign also carries a disclaimer that use of his military rank and photographs does not imply Navy endorsement [19]. On Iowa Press he said he grew up Republican and knocked doors for John McCain in 2008, and that "the party's left me more than I've left the party" [5].

### Track record
Peterman has never held elected office, so he has no voting or governing record [3]. His professional record is his Navy service, described above. As a candidate, he filed four public records requests in July 2026 seeking details of the data Iowa gave the DOJ [8]. He says he has visited all 99 counties and met with county auditors "in small towns and big cities" (self-reported) [5][19]. His campaign has scheduled October town halls in Audubon, Crawford and Monona counties [19]. In July he filed four public records requests about what Iowa gave the DOJ. His Aug. 15 State Fair Soapbox speech called for a secretary of state who "knows who they work for" [8]. His campaign page lists an ad titled "Always Protect Our Personal Information" [19].

### What they're running on
His campaign describes the job as making sure "every eligible Iowan can vote, that elections are fair and secure, and that politics never gets in the way." He says he is "not running to be a partisan warrior" (campaign, self-reported) [19]. His main themes are protecting voters' personal data, expanding access to voting, and nonpartisan administration [18][8]. His specific proposals:
- **Refuse federal data demands:** He would oppose "illegal requests" for voter data and says he "would not have complied" with the DOJ request [3][5].
- **Longer early voting:** He would ask the Legislature to make the early-voting window "as long as we can" [8]. He argues the 20-day window does not improve security and makes voting less accessible, partly because mail service is slow in some areas [8][14].
- **Restore attestation:** He would advocate restoring voter attestation, which lets a registered voter vouch for another voter's identity. A 2026 law (HF 2501) removed it, and he says there was "no evidence of fraud being committed because of attestation" [8][27].
- **Non-English materials:** He opposes the ban on non-English ballots and registration forms [9].
- **Election Day holiday:** He would make Election Day a state holiday, partly to free up more people to work the polls [5].
- **No late voter-roll actions:** He says "purges" like the one in 2024 should not come "at the last minute" [5].
- **Transparency on federal monitors:** After Pate's Oct. 28 event he said, "Secretary Pate owes Iowans answers," and asked where the monitors will be deployed, why, and what the office is doing to keep them from interfering with voters [15].
- **Non-English ballots:** He opposes the ban on non-English ballots and registration forms, which he says makes voting harder [9]. The Iowa Supreme Court rejected LULAC's challenge to the English-only rules in May 2025 [28]. He has not described a specific change he could make himself, and at the Soapbox he said he was "not so naive as to believe that I can unilaterally change that" in the case of early voting [8].
- **Priorities beyond elections:** He also talks about cost of living, water quality, public education and health-care clinic closures, though the office does not control those [5][18]. He told the N'West Iowa REVIEW that his focus is "serving Iowans, not serving party bosses, not serving the DOJ or the White House" [18].

### Positions on key issues
- **Voter ID:** "I do support voter ID" [5].
- **Noncitizen voting:** He says it is illegal and he would "hold anyone accountable" [5].
- **Vote by mail:** He supports it but recommends voting early in person when possible [5].
- **Open primaries and approval voting:** He is open to them but "cautious." He says they are questions for the Legislature, as long as any change is "not exclusionary" [5].
- **Recount law:** He says county auditors report positive experiences with the post-2020 changes. He pledges to certify results "however the votes land" [5].
- **Security:** He cites his experience assessing risk as a pilot for both physical and cyber threats [5].

### Endorsements & funding
- **Endorsements:** Ballotpedia lists the Iowa Federation of Labor, AFL-CIO and International Union of Operating Engineers Local 150, plus several small "Sway" voting groups [22]. The Scott County Democrats list him as a party-endorsed candidate (advocacy; search excerpt only) [23]. His campaign page has an "Endorsed by" logo whose endorser could not be identified [19]. No newspaper editorial endorsement was found.
- **Fundraising:** His July 2026 report showed $51,908 raised and $168,695 on hand [18]. Iowa Capital Dispatch reported the figure as "more than $166,000" [17]. Per the Des Moines Register, his October report showed $184,690 raised and $185,110 on hand, more than Pate [16]. A campaign press release reposted by a local outlet said he outraised Pate by nearly $140,000 in an earlier period (not independently checked) [33]. His donations run through ActBlue (campaign, self-reported) [19]. Peterman says he is outraising Pate and "feeling good" about his chances [18].

### Criticisms & responses
- **Off-topic campaigning.** When Peterman tied the race to tariffs, the cost of living, an "unnecessary war in Iran," clinic closures and water quality, Pate replied, "It sounds like you're running for Congress." Peterman responded that this "makes it seem like voters don't have a role" in holding leaders accountable [5].
- **Party record on voter ID.** Pate said the national Democratic Party opposes voter ID [5]. Peterman says he personally supports it [5].
- **Experience.** No detailed published criticism of Peterman's lack of election-administration experience was found. Pate's own description of himself as a seasoned "referee" implies the contrast [4][5]. Peterman cites his nonpartisan officer service, his work with auditors, and his Senate fellowship as preparation [5][19]. He says that as Secretary he is "required to be nonpartisan" [5].

## The voter-data dispute: both sides
- **What happened.** On May 5, 2026, Pate confirmed that Iowa had given the U.S. Justice Department (DOJ) its voter registration data, including driver's license numbers and Social Security numbers [6]. Pate's own May 5 release says the U.S. Attorney General may demand data that includes driver's license and Social Security numbers, and that the list was "securely transferred"; it does not itemize the fields sent [25]. On Iowa Press, reporter Stephen Gruber-Miller described the data as including "driver's license numbers, partial Social Security numbers," and Pate defended the decision without correcting that description [5]. Separately, State Sen. Janet Peterson (D) said a Feb.-March email from Deputy Secretary of State Eric Gookin said such elements were not sent, while a March 5 Pate letter to the DOJ appeared to show sensitive data was included [26]. The office has not published an itemized list of fields.
- **Pate's case.** His office's lawyers and the Attorney General's office concluded that the U.S. Attorney General can demand the data under the federal Civil Rights Act. They also concluded that the federal Driver's Privacy Protection Act allows government agencies to share license data, and that these federal laws override Iowa's limits [6]. "We are providing this information to the Department of Justice because we must comply with federal law," he said [6]. He says he refused to sign the DOJ's memorandum of understanding and instead set his own conditions on how the data may be used [7][5]. He notes that most voter-list data is already available to parties and candidates [5]. He said he decided based on the information he had at the time, before the court cases, and called his critics "Monday morning quarterbacks" [7]. In October he said, "now that we see some of the court cases, it would have been a factor in our decision" [5].
- **Peterman's case.** He calls the decision "indefensible" and says about 30 states refused the same request [6][5]. Iowa Capital Dispatch, citing the Brennan Center, reported that the DOJ sued those states and that 20 of the suits had been dismissed as of August 2026 [7]. Peterman says the DOJ has not won any of them [5]. He says a Secretary of State must have "judgment at the time the decision needs to be made" and that he "would not have complied" [5]. He filed four public records requests about what was shared [8]. Democratic Attorney General candidate Nate Willems also criticized Attorney General Bird's office for approving the transfer [6].

**Where they differ most.** The biggest difference is the 2026 handover of voter data to the DOJ. Pate says he acted on legal advice and under conditions he set. Peterman says the request could and should have been refused. They also differ on 2024's late-season challenges to suspected noncitizens: Pate defends them as necessary at the time, and Peterman calls them a last-minute purge. On voting access, Peterman proposes restoring attestation, ending the non-English materials ban, and making Election Day a holiday. Pate has not taken those positions and opposes the holiday. They overlap on several points: both support voter ID, both want a longer early-voting window than 20 days (Pate proposes 5 more days, Peterman a longer but unspecified expansion), and both describe the job as nonpartisan.
