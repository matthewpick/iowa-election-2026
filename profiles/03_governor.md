# Iowa Governor / Lieutenant Governor — Candidate Profiles

_What the office does:_ The governor is Iowa's chief executive and serves a four-year term; the salary is $130,000 [1]. The governor and lieutenant governor run as a joint ticket [2]. Under Article IV of the Iowa Constitution the "supreme executive power" is vested in the governor; the governor and lieutenant governor are elected jointly, the lieutenant governor has the duties provided by law plus any the governor assigns, and the lieutenant governor takes over if the governor cannot serve [27]. The Legislature's own guide to the executive branch says the governor can call special sessions and veto acts of the General Assembly [28].

_Ballot order / candidates:_ Zach Lahn / Derek Wulf (Republican); Rob Sand / Dave Muhlbauer (Democratic). Open seat: Republican Gov. Kim Reynolds, in office since 2017, is not running [6]. A Libertarian ticket was removed from the ballot on June 16 for a paperwork defect [2].

## Zach Lahn (Republican)

### At a glance
- Age 40; farmer and businessman from Belle Plaine [1].
- Grew up near Sioux City; B.A. in political science, University of Colorado (2009) [1][14].
- Previously worked for Montana and Colorado congressmen and as Montana director and a fundraiser for Americans for Prosperity (AFP) [13]. The Capital Dispatch describes his experience as working for a Colorado state senator and running Republican campaigns in Iowa [1].
- Co-founder of the investment firm Homeplace Ventures and of Wonder, a private pre-K–12 school in Wichita, Kansas [14].
- First run for public office. Won the June 2 primary with 37.8% (80,407 votes) over Rep. Randy Feenstra at 37.1% (78,763), Adam Steen, Brad Sherman and Eddie Andrews [2].

### Background & career
Lahn operates the family farm in Belle Plaine, which he bought in 2013 per KMAland [14]. The Kansas Reflector reports the Belle Plaine homestead was bought in 2014 with his previous wife and that he farmed it as a sixth-generation Iowan [13]; the two accounts differ on the year. His campaign says he farms with regenerative practices and has helped restore the King Theater in Belle Plaine (self-reported) [9].

After AFP, Lahn moved to Wichita to launch Wonder, which opened in 2018 on the Wichita State campus and was financed by Chase Koch, son of Charles Koch, and his then-wife Annie Koch. Lahn later married Annie Koch; they have seven children in a blended family [13]. The Gazette calls him a "venture firm executive" [6]. He loaned his campaign $2 million before the primary [13].

Residency is a recurring campaign topic. Kansas voter records show Lahn voted in Kansas in 2018, 2020 and 2022; he registered in Iowa on Oct. 17, 2024, which met the two-year requirement [13]. He told the Des Moines Register he moved to Iowa in 2023 and flew his plane between Iowa and Wichita 37 times since Oct. 1, 2025 to see children from earlier marriages [13].

His own submitted biography to Ballotpedia describes him as "a father, a farmer, and a businessman" who is "my own biggest donor" and says, "I don't answer to donors or corporations — only to God, my family, and the people of this state" (self-reported) [2]. His campaign site says he is a sixth-generation Iowan whose ancestor rode with Iowa's Second Cavalry in 1862, and that he and his wife Annie have seven children (self-reported) [9].

### Track record
Lahn has never held elected office. His professional record is the AFP work, the Wonder school and Homeplace Ventures described above, and farming [13][14]. The Kansas Reflector characterizes his campaign as outsider and populist, and reports he denounces lobbyists and corporations while criticizing both parties for blocking reform; he calls this fighting the "Uni-party" [13]. No independent evaluation of Wonder's outcomes or of Homeplace Ventures' holdings was found.

### What they're running on
Self-reported themes: Free Iowa's Farmers; Make Iowa Healthy Again; restore the education system [2]. His site adds "Iowa First" values (protect life, end state benefits for people in the country illegally, keep politics out of schools), keeping Iowa land in Iowa hands, and affordability including banning hedge funds from buying single-family homes [9][10].

Primary positions that carried into the general election, per his campaign and press accounts:
- Higher property taxes on land owned by out-of-state investors to lower taxes on Iowa farms [10][13].
- A promised veto of any bill giving agricultural chemical companies immunity from failure-to-warn lawsuits [13].
- Support for ESAs, with an emphasis on teacher "innovation zones" and removing teachers who push political views [10][13]. In the primary he said, "We don't have a spending problem. We have a quality problem" [13].
- Cancer as a central issue, backed by MAHA Action [13]. In his general-election plan he calls for independent state safety testing of agricultural products and consumer products, with results published and "no corporate influence," and for pointing University of Iowa and Iowa State research at cancer (self-reported) [9][10].
- A "radical transparency" proposal for state spending. In the final debate he floated blockchain tools that would create "thousands of citizen auditors," starting at the state level [8].
- Economic development: he supports the $1.4 billion MEGA incentive package for the Mesabi Metallics steel mill in Lee County, calling it "transformational" for southeast Iowa and saying the governor should be "the chief negotiator for the state" [3]. He opposes incentives for data centers ("a $100 billion multinational company does not need welfare from the people of Iowa") and cited one center with $529 million in property tax abatements for 30 jobs [8].
- Workforce and schools: prioritizing Iowans for state jobs and university admissions, skilled-trades training, local-sourced school food starting with protein [5][10].

### Positions on key issues
- **Property taxes:** Wants to lower the burden; long-term goal is eliminating property tax on single-family primary homes. At the final debate he said a roughly 2.5-cent sales tax increase, offered by someone who talked to him, could offset it; he called the idea not simple [6][7]. Wants a new, higher property tax class for data centers [8]. Said he is not proposing increases to major taxes [7]; defends recent income tax cuts [4].
- **ESA program:** Supports universal eligibility ("not a welfare program. This is an opportunity fund") and independent third-party audits; opposes requiring private schools to take every applicant [4][8].
- **Water quality and cancer:** Says nitrates are a top cause of several cancers; favors water monitoring, targeted incentives for most-affected waterways (wetlands, saturated buffers, cover crops), funding the voter-approved IWILL trust fund, and opposes new mandates on farmers [4][6][14].
- **Abortion:** Says the "lives of the unborn" should be protected; his site lists "protect life" [9][10][8]. He has not, in sources found, endorsed repealing or loosening the six-week ban.
- **Medicaid:** Keep privately managed Medicaid with stronger oversight (timely payments, limits on improper denials); supports work or volunteer requirements [4][7].
- **Minimum wage:** Did not call for raising it above $7.25; favors incentives for Iowa-based employers [6].
- **Cannabis:** Supports expanding medical marijuana, opposes recreational legalization. He told reporters Oct. 28 the program is "far too restrictive" and that he is "not in favor of full legalization" [24].
- **Other:** Would sign no new firearm restrictions; says the state is "in a good place" [8]. Campaign site pledges to end state benefits for people in the country illegally and to ban hedge funds from buying single-family homes (self-reported) [9][10].

### Endorsements & funding
Self-reported endorsements: MAHA Action, Standing for Health Freedom, former Rep. Steve King, and several Republican state representatives including Wulf [2]. MAHA Action announced a $1.5 million donation on Oct. 15 [20]. Lahn appeared with Sen. Chuck Grassley on Oct. 13 and at a Trump rally in Sioux City on Oct. 22 [per C-SPAN and Fox captions, 8].

Funding: Raised $16.87 million July 15–Oct. 14, spent $17.5 million, had about $403,000 on hand, and listed $2.5 million in loans [20]. Capital Dispatch reports $11.8 million of the period's receipts came from Right Direction PAC, tied to the Republican Governors Association [21]. Cycle total through Oct. 14 was reported as $26.94 million [21][39]. In the final pre-election report he raised $5.9 million, bringing his cycle total to $32.8 million (Register, excerpt only) [37]. Earlier reports: about $163,000 raised through May 14, then $6.37 million from May 15 to July 14 [2]. Lahn has said he is the largest donor to his own campaign (self-reported) [2], and the Kansas Reflector reported a $2 million personal loan before the primary [13].

### Criticisms & responses
- **Residency.** Sand and Gov. Laura Kelly (D-KS) say Lahn is tied to Kansas; Kelly's video said "it is time to come back to Kansas" [3]. Lahn calls this "a central lie of Rob Sand's campaign," says he is a sixth-generation Iowan and the issue concerns his children [3][23].
- **Wulf selection.** Sand questioned how Lahn vetted his running mate (see Wulf section) [17].
- **Curriculum ad and public schools.** In the first debate Sand said, "I don't think you're going to be the biggest advocate for public school students over a guy who sends his kids there," referring to ads saying Marxists had hijacked Iowa's curriculum. Lahn's response in that exchange was the residency rebuttal above; his stated position is that the governor should be public education's top supporter and that teachers should not push political doctrine [14][23].
- **IWILL vote.** Sand said he voted for the 2010 conservation fund "because I lived here. You didn't." Lahn answered that he is a sixth-generation Iowan who lives in Iowa [23]. Both now support funding IWILL [6].
- **Kansas ties and travel.** The Iowa Democratic Party called him a "Kansas carpetbagger." Lahn's campaign did not respond to the Kansas Reflector's questions about why he did not run in Kansas [13]; Lahn told the Register his arrangement would be "different" as governor [3].
- **Campaign-trail claim about Sand's budget.** Not a criticism of Lahn; see Sand section.

## Rob Sand (Democrat)

### At a glance
- Age 44; Iowa State Auditor since Jan. 2019; lives in Des Moines with his wife Christine and two sons [1][18].
- Born and raised in Decorah; Brown University; University of Iowa law (merit scholarship) [1][18].
- Assistant Iowa Attorney General 2010–2017 [1].
- Ran unopposed in the Democratic primary (99.7%) [2]. The only Democrat now holding statewide office [15].

### Background & career
The Auditor of State's biography, which Sand's office publishes, says he spent seven years as the Attorney General's office chief public-corruption prosecutor [18]. He led the discovery and prosecution of the Hot Lotto lottery-rigging scheme, which the Capital Dispatch puts at $25 million [1][18]. He also prosecuted the Iowa Film Office tax credit scandal, a Ponzi-scheme-related case and "nearly two dozen" sexually violent predator cases, per the office [18]. He wrote a book about Hot Lotto, "The Winning Ticket" (self-reported) [2]. He was elected auditor in 2018 over the incumbent Republican and re-elected in 2022 (self-reported) [2].

### Track record
- **Audits and reports.** The office states that it found Gov. Reynolds misspent $21 million of CARES Act money on contracts unrelated to COVID-19 management, after which the state returned the money to its proper fund; and that a joint FBI investigation found a Metro Waste Authority operations director defrauded the agency of more than $2 million [18]. The office also says it won a unanimous Iowa Supreme Court decision to obtain documents on the largest financial deal in state history [18].
- **PIE program.** Sand created the Public Innovations and Efficiencies program, which gives awards to counties, cities and school districts that adopt tax-saving practices [18]. The auditor's site hosts a PIE checklist for state and local governments and a January 2022 news release titled "Mississippi Replicates Iowa Auditor's PIE Program" [29]. His campaign says he would make it mandatory across state agencies and that Mississippi's Republican auditor copied it (self-reported) [12]. No dollar total of PIE savings could be confirmed from the pages reviewed.
- **Other reports.** The auditor's site lists 2026 reports including "Executive Branch Employee Salaries and Employment Trends," an "ESA Policy Impact Report," and "Nursing Home Inspections and Staffing"; their contents were not reviewed [30].
- **Statewide Work, Statewide Jobs.** His office lets employees live anywhere in Iowa [11][18].
- **2023 access law (Senate File 478).** The bill limits the auditor's access to certain personal information and sends disputes with agencies to a three-member arbitration board, with one member chosen by the governor. It passed the Senate 33-16 in April 2023 and was signed in June 2023 [19][18]. Sen. Mike Bousselot (R-Ankeny) said the Legislature acts as "watchdog of the auditor" after Supreme Court subpoena rulings and that "the watchdog still has teeth" [19]. Sand called it "the greatest pro-corruption bill and the worst perversion of checks and balances in Iowa's history" [19]. The National State Auditors Association, the AICPA and former Comptroller General David Walker opposed it [18][19]. Sand now proposes restoring the office's full powers [12].

### What they're running on
Self-reported "Truer Better" agenda: accountability (ban stock trading by officials, term and age limits, mandatory prison for stealing over $10,000 in public money, whistleblower protections, annual town halls); restoring Auditor powers; opening primaries and approval voting; universal all-day pre-K; ending Medicaid privatization; legal cannabis; a $12 minimum wage; and a social media age limit under 16 [1][11][12][26]. He describes himself as a reluctant Democrat running to make Iowa "truer and better, not redder or bluer" [6][15].

His Ballotpedia survey statement (self-reported) frames the race as accountability: "Our current elected officials have led Iowa down the wrong path for years, landing us in dead last for economic growth, middle of the pack for public education, and number one for cancer growth." It also says Iowa faces a deficit of nearly $1.4 billion and promises to "reign in reckless spending" on day one [2]. In the final debate he called the state's spending gap, which he put above $1 billion, a "fiscal time bomb" [4]. His site proposes free school breakfast and lunch to draw federal funds, universal pre-K paid for by ESA changes, and moving state jobs to rural areas [5][11].

### Positions on key issues
- **Property taxes:** On the 2026 property tax law, said he wants to "live with this law for a minute" and see its effects before adjusting [6][7]. Proposes higher rates on out-of-state landowners [2]. Says data centers should pay "all their taxes and then some" [8].
- **ESA program:** Wants income limits restored, annual audits, tuition-increase limits, and an open-admission requirement for participating private schools; says the changes would address about half the state's budget gap together with data center and cannabis revenue [4][12].
- **Water quality and cancer:** Says nitrates contribute to cancer; proposes ending conservation waiting lists, a large wetlands restoration through reverse auction, and funding IWILL; relies on voluntary participation. Also proposes cancer research investment [2][4][6].
- **Abortion:** Calls Iowa's six-week ban "incredibly restrictive," says "I would veto going further" and would veto "culture war" bills [8]. He did not, in sources reviewed, state a plan to repeal the ban.
- **Medicaid:** Would reverse privatization by executive order; supports work requirements but "not a paperwork requirement" [4][11][6].
- **Minimum wage:** $12 an hour, up from $7.25 [6][26].
- **Cannabis:** Legalize, tax and regulate adult-use cannabis, citing revenue [24][12].

### Endorsements & funding
Raised $16.31 million July 15–Oct. 14, spent $28.9 million, had $5.35 million on hand and no debt; the Democratic Governors Association gave $5.5 million [20]. Cycle total through Oct. 14: $54.15 million [21][39]. His campaign says it raised $4.8 million Oct. 15–27 and had $1.3 million on hand heading into Election Day (self-reported) [38]; the Register's Oct. 31 summary puts his cycle total at about $59 million (excerpt only) [37]. Earlier reports: $9.57 million raised through May 14 and $9.39 million from May 15 to July 14 [2]. His campaign says it received more than 88,000 contributions from all 99 counties [20]. Kentucky Gov. Andy Beshear campaigned with him and Kelly endorsed him [3][15]. A full endorsement list was not compiled.

### Criticisms & responses
- **"Not really nonpartisan."** Lahn and Iowa GOP Chair Jeff Kaufmann point to Sand's donations from prominent Democrats and to the Iowa Democratic Party, with Kaufmann calling his framing "phony crap." Sand spokesperson Maddie Moher responded that he has worked with all parties and called Lahn "a career political operative" [22].
- **Budget in first year.** Lahn said Sand overspent and needed a bailout from the governor; Sand said the office is funded largely by client payments and that his predecessor had burned through its accounts [3].
- **Wealth and family ties.** Lahn pointed to properties and businesses of Sand's wife's family, the Lauridsen Group. Sand said "my in-laws' business doesn't sell nitrate fertilizer" and did not otherwise respond directly [3][23].
- **Taxes.** Lahn said Sand opposed tax cuts "at every step" and "Rob is supporting the raising of taxes because that's what liberals do"; Sand denied wanting to raise taxes [23]. Sand's proposals to tax cannabis and change data center tax treatment are his stated revenue sources [4].
- **ESA oversight.** Lahn opposes a requirement that private schools admit all applicants, saying it would burden small schools; he proposes third-party audits to protect school privacy [8]. Sand says schools taking public money "should have to take whichever students say they want to attend" [8].
- **Auditor's office funding.** See above; Lahn's claim was made at the first debate and Sand's rebuttal is quoted there [3].

## Running mates

### Derek Wulf (R, lieutenant governor)
Wulf, of Hudson, has represented House District 76 since January 2023 after defeating Democrat Kate Wyatt in 2022. He has a B.S. in animal science from Iowa State and worked on the family farm [16]. He chairs the Agriculture Committee and sits on Natural Resources, State Government and Ways and Means [16]. Lahn said he chose Wulf for his work restricting eminent domain for carbon-capture pipelines and leading farm-equipment right-to-repair legislation [Capital Dispatch via search excerpt, 16]. A 2022 residency complaint to the Ethics Board was dismissed [16]. The Legislature's page shows he chaired Natural Resources and sat on Agriculture from Jan. 13 to Oct. 9, 2025, and now chairs Agriculture [31]. Reported bills: he introduced and floor-managed a 2026 bill shielding farmers and ethanol producers from greenhouse-gas emission lawsuits, calling it an "ag friendly bill" that lets farmers "continue to do what they do"; the House approved it Feb. 26, and Iowa Capital Dispatch and E&E News describe him as its House sponsor [32][33]. The House passed a separate carbon-pipeline eminent-domain bill, House File 2104, 64-28 on Jan. 21, 2026; Lahn cited Wulf's eminent-domain work, but Wulf's specific role in HF 2104 was not confirmed [32][34]. Right-to-repair for farm equipment also passed the House in April 2026 [per Lahn announcement, 1].

Controversy: the Des Moines Register reported in September that the Black Hawk County Sheriff's Office was investigating fraud allegations by a lender, WCF Financial, involving Wulf's farm business. The sheriff sent findings to the county attorney; no charges had been filed as of Sept. 22 [16][17]. Lahn disputed that Wulf is under criminal investigation and said "nobody's going to lose a dime"; his campaign said Wulf "has not broken the law" [17]. KCCI reported on Oct. 22 that Wulf's parents filed a petition in Black Hawk County alleging he owes about $275,000 in unpaid farmland rent since 2024; they said they filed to protect their assets as a bank seeks to foreclose on his other debts. Wulf had said his refinancing was complete and would not affect the campaign [36].  Wulf's voting record was not reviewed.

### Dave Muhlbauer (D, lieutenant governor)
Muhlbauer is a fifth-generation farmer and cattleman in western Iowa, serving his second term on the Crawford County Board of Supervisors [15]. He sought the Democratic nomination for U.S. Senate in 2022 but exited in 2021 before the primary [15]; Ballotpedia lists the candidacy as withdrawn [2]. His father, Dan Muhlbauer, served in the Iowa House 2011–2014 and his grandfather Louis in the Legislature 1983–1992 [15]. He described his family in 2021 as "old-school, farming labor Democrats" [15]. Sand's campaign biography (self-reported) says he returned home after college to farm, attended IKM-Manning schools and briefly Wartburg College, and lives with his wife Linda; they have three children. Quoting it, Muhlbauer says "you don't ask who they voted for" when a neighbor needs help baling hay [35]. A Muhlbauer-Sand event record, or any votes he cast as a supervisor, was not found; the campaign says only that he works "with everyone to deliver for western Iowa" [35]. Sand said he picked him as "somebody I knew I could trust" and for his record working across party lines [15]; Lahn's side has not, in sources found, criticized his supervisor record. The Senate withdrawal is the only run beyond local office found in the sources reviewed; earlier legislative runs could not be verified (see Open questions).

### Debates
Three debates took place. Oct. 6 (KWQC, Davenport) was dominated by residency, family wealth and the Mesabi Metallics steel mill incentive package; Sand favored a benchmark-based approach and Lahn called it transformational for southeast Iowa [3]. Oct. 14 (KTIV, Sioux City) was less combative; both said the other was a good father, and they discussed ESAs, local school food and workforce [5]. Oct. 27 (KCRG/The Gazette, Cedar Rapids) was a 90-minute event described as cordial; topics included property taxes, mental health, Medicaid work requirements, water, ESAs, data centers, the minimum wage and guns [4][6][7][8]. A scheduled Oct. 21 Iowa PBS "Iowa Press" debate was not confirmed in sources reviewed [3].

### Where they differ most
They differ on whether to keep the ESA program open to all families or restore income limits, on whether Medicaid stays privately managed, on raising the $7.25 minimum wage, and on recreational cannabis. On property taxes Lahn proposes a long-term end to home property taxes while Sand favors waiting to assess the 2026 law. Both say they oppose tax incentives for data centers, favor mental health capacity, back work requirements for Medicaid, support funding IWILL and favor voluntary conservation. Both pledged to protect Second Amendment rights; Sand supports background checks and Lahn would sign no new firearm restrictions [8].
